# How to Use Surfer SEO for Content Briefs: A Complete Walkthrough

Your content ranks on page one because the brief was built on real data, not guesswork. That is the outcome writers and SEO strategists get when they stop winging their outlines and start using [Surfer SEO](https://surferseo.com/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) to build content briefs that actually match search intent. I have tested this workflow across dozens of articles, and the difference in ranking speed is not subtle.

This guide walks you through the exact process I use.

---

## What You Need Before Starting

You need an active [Surfer SEO](https://surferseo.com/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) account. The Basic plan works for this workflow, but the Growth plan gives you more daily Content Editor credits, which matters if you are briefing multiple pieces per week.

You also need your target keyword ready. Do not skip keyword research before opening Surfer. I use [SEMrush](https://semrush.com/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) to validate search volume and keyword difficulty before I spend a Surfer credit on a Content Editor report.

Finally, decide who is writing the content. If you are handing off to a freelancer, you will export the brief as a shareable link or PDF. If you are writing it yourself or using an AI tool like [Jasper](https://jasper.ai/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) or [Writesonic](https://writesonic.com/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog), you can work directly inside the Surfer editor.

## Step-by-Step Walkthrough

### Step 1: Open the Content Editor

Log into Surfer SEO and click **Content Editor** in the left sidebar. Click **Create Content Editor**. This is where your brief will live.

### Step 2: Enter Your Target Keyword

Type your primary keyword into the search field. Select your target country and language. Click **Create**. Surfer will analyze the top-ranking pages for that keyword and return within 30 to 60 seconds.

### Step 3: Review the Content Score Targets

Once the editor loads, look at the right-hand panel. You will see a target word count range, a recommended number of headings, images, and paragraphs. These are pulled from top-ranking competitors. I treat these as floors, not ceilings.

### Step 4: Study the Outline Suggestions

Click the **Outline** tab in the right panel. Surfer generates suggested headings pulled from competing pages. Go through each one. Not every suggestion will be relevant, and some will be redundant. Select the headings that make logical sense for your article structure.

Do not just accept all suggestions blindly. I reject at least 20 to 30 percent of them every time because they do not match the angle I am taking.

### Step 5: Build the Heading Structure

Drag the accepted headings into the editor in the order that makes sense for your reader journey. Organize them as H2s and H3s. Think about flow first, keyword density second.

At this point you have a skeleton. The next steps fill it with context for your writer.

### Step 6: Check the NLP Terms Panel

Click the **Terms** tab. Surfer shows you a list of keywords and NLP phrases that top-ranking pages use. These are color-coded: green means you have hit the target frequency, orange means you are close, red means you have not used the term yet.

At the brief stage, I copy the high-priority red and orange terms into a notes section for the writer. I tell them which terms need to appear and roughly how many times.

### Step 7: Analyze the Top Competitors Manually

Click the **SERP Analyzer** or open competitor URLs directly from the Content Editor. I open the top three ranking pages in separate tabs. Read the introductions, check subheading angles, and note any unique data or examples they cite.

This step is not automated. It takes 10 to 15 minutes and it is where you find gaps your article can exploit.

### Step 8: Write the Brief Summary

Now compile everything into a brief document. I paste the Surfer outline into a Google Doc and add these sections below each heading:

- A one-to-two sentence description of what that section should cover
- Relevant NLP terms to hit in that section
- Any data points or examples the writer should reference
- Internal linking suggestions

This combination of Surfer's data and your editorial direction is what separates a good brief from a generic one.

### Step 9: Set the Content Score Target

Go back to the Surfer editor. Note the target content score range shown in the top right, typically between 67 and 85 depending on the keyword. Tell your writer to aim for the midpoint of that range before submitting.

If you are using [Copy.ai](https://copy.ai/referral/chronostrail&utm_source=blog) or [Jasper](https://jasper.ai/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) to generate a draft, paste the output into the Surfer editor immediately and check the score before editing. This saves time on revision passes.

### Step 10: Share the Brief

Click **Share** in the top right of the Content Editor. You get a shareable link that gives your writer read-only or full editing access to the Surfer environment. Paste this link into your Google Doc brief. Your writer can now write directly in Surfer and hit the score target in real time.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Using Surfer without validating the keyword first.** Surfer tells you how to rank, not whether you should target a keyword. Always check volume and competition in a tool like [SEMrush](https://semrush.com/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) before spending a credit.

**Accepting every outline suggestion.** Surfer pulls headings from competitors, which means it also pulls mediocre structure from mediocre articles. You need editorial judgment to filter the results.

**Obsessing over the content score.** A score of 85 does not guarantee a ranking. I have ranked articles in the 60s and failed to rank articles in the 90s. The score is a signal, not a guarantee.

**Skipping the competitor analysis step.** The automated data is useful. Reading the actual pages is better. You find angles, gaps, and tone signals that no tool surfaces automatically.

**Writing vague section notes in the brief.** Telling a writer to "cover the topic thoroughly" is useless. Give them specific talking points, example questions to answer, and terms to include.

## Pro Tips

**Use Surfer's Topical Map feature before briefing a new cluster.** If you are building out a content pillar, the Topical Map shows you which supporting articles to create so your cluster has full coverage. Brief those articles in the right order to build topical authority faster.

**Pair Surfer with an AI writer for first drafts.** I run the Surfer outline through [Writesonic](https://writesonic.com/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) or [Jasper](https://jasper.ai/?via=chronostrail&utm_source=blog) to generate a rough draft, then paste it into the Content Editor to check the score. The AI handles volume, the editor handles optimization, and I handle the editorial layer.

**Save your brief templates.** Once you have a brief format that works, save it as a Google Doc template. Every new Surfer report slots into the same structure. This cuts brief production time in half after the first few runs.

**Brief for search intent, not just keywords.** Surfer shows you what ranks. It does not tell you why. Before you finalize the brief angle, confirm whether the top results are informational, commercial, or transactional. Match your angle to intent or your content score will not save you.

**Refresh your reports for evergreen content.** If an article has been live for six months and is stalling on page two, regenerate the Surfer report and compare it to your current content. The competitive landscape shifts, and your brief targets from six months ago may no longer reflect what Google is rewarding.
